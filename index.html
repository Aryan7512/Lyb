Context research

Is energy the right target?

The brief calls energy Plant X’s largest controllable cost. We checked where process-plant energy goes, what can be fixed without AI, and whether carbon now has a price in India.

Option ranking → slide 1

Steam is where chemical-sector energy goes

US DOE steam sourcebook
Source shows

Steam generation accounted for about 57% of the total energy used by the US chemicals industry S7

→↓
Design choice

The pilot starts in utilities (boilers, steam and compressed air), where the energy is concentrated.

CaveatBased on 1994 data, so we treat it as directional only.

Simple boiler fixes don’t need AI

US DOE Steam Tip Sheet #4
Source shows

Boiler efficiency rises by about 1% for every 15% reduction in excess air, or for every 40°F reduction in stack temperature S6

→↓
Design choice

Single-loop fixes (O2 trim, steam traps, leaks) are done without AI. AI is used only for cross-unit trade-offs that no single loop can see.

Carbon now has a market in India

Carbon Credit Trading Scheme
Source shows

India’s Carbon Credit Trading Scheme was notified by the Ministry of Power on 28 June 2023 and covers 9 energy-intensive sectors, including petrochemicals S8

Amendment rules in force from 13 Jan 2026 set GHG emission-intensity targets for petrochemical crackers, with baseline FY 2023-24 and compliance years 2025-26 and 2026-27 S9 Secondary

→↓
Design choice

Avoided CO2 now has market value, so emissions are tracked alongside cost rather than treated as a side benefit.

Converting electricity savings to CO2

Central Electricity Authority
Source shows

The Indian grid emission factor was 0.716 tCO2/MWh for FY 2022-23 S10

→↓
Design choice

This is the factor we use to convert electricity savings into avoided CO2.

Real operators already fund efficiency

LyondellBasell, Feb 2026
Source shows

LyondellBasell’s Scope 1+2 target is now −32% by 2030 versus 2020 (revised from 42%), with energy-efficiency projects run through its Value Enhancement Program and at least 50% renewable electricity by 2030 S11 Trade press

→↓
Design choice

Efficiency is a lever that real operators already fund, so Option E is a realistic choice. Plant X itself is fictional and is not modelled on any real site.

Case studies and patterns

Has this worked before?

Four published cases, from data centres to distillation columns to cement. They share a path from AI advice to supervised AI control, and they set the ceiling that our 4% sits well under.

4% assumption (A3) → slide 1

Google / DeepMind: from advice to supervised control

Data-centre cooling, 2016–2018
Source shows

A 2016 recommendation-only system was useful but too labour-intensive for operators to implement. By 2018 the AI controlled cooling directly under operator supervision, saving ~30% on average after a few months S1

Safety design: uncertainty estimates drop low-confidence actions, two layers of checks against operator-set limits, automatic fallback, and human override always wins S1

→↓
Design choice

Advice is a stage, not the end state, so Plant X plans a staged move towards control.

We copy the safety pattern: discard low-confidence moves, check every move against engineer-set limits, fall back automatically, and let operator override always win.

Yokogawa + JSR: autonomous AI on a real distillation column

Chemical plant, 2022–2023
Source shows

A reinforcement-learning AI (FKDPP) ran a distillation column autonomously for 35 days (840 h, 17 Jan–21 Feb 2022), a claimed world first. It was built and tested on a simulator first, ran inside the existing control system with all interlocks kept, and was checked for acceptance by veteran operators S2

After about a year it was formally adopted at ENEOS Materials, which reported 40% less steam and CO2 than manual control on that column S3

→↓
Design choice

Prove the model offline before it touches the plant. Run it inside the existing control system, never around it, and keep every interlock.

Treat acceptance by experienced operators as a pass/fail check.

CaveatBoth figures come from the vendor’s own press releases and cover a single column, not a whole site.

McKinsey: AI optimisers in cement plants

Feldmann, Dec 2019
Source shows

An AI real-time optimiser runs either in advisory mode (operators make the adjustments) or in autonomous mode. One plant saw up to a 10% gain in throughput and energy efficiency. Scaling depends on change management S4

→↓
Design choice

Build both modes from day one so the site can move between them. Plan and budget for change management, not only software.

ISO 50001: gains last when they become routine

Fitzgerald et al., 2023 · 83 facilities
Source shows

Across 83 manufacturing facilities, energy performance improved by ~4.1% in year 1 and was still improving by ~3.4% a year after 12 years, because energy management became part of daily operations S5

→↓
Design choice

The optimiser has to live inside daily shift work, through the operator console and a weekly savings review, rather than run as a one-off project.

Human-factors research

Will operators use it?

The brief’s catch for Option E is that production teams fear automated set-point changes. Research on people and automation shaped how the operator console behaves.

How the catch is handled → slide 2

Two ways automation fails people: misuse and disuse

Parasuraman & Riley, 1997
Source shows

People can misuse automation by relying on it too much, or disuse it by ignoring automation that works well S12

→↓
Design choice

We plan for both: newer operators may over-trust the optimiser, and experienced ones may reject it.

Design for appropriate reliance

Lee & See, 2004
Source shows

Trust should match what the automation can actually do. Making the automation’s reasoning visible helps operators rely on it the right amount S13

→↓
Design choice

Every recommendation shows its reason and its ₹ saving. Rejections are logged with a reason and reviewed, so we learn where operators disagree.

The ironies of automation

Bainbridge, 1983
Source shows

Automation wears away the very skills operators need for the rare moments when they must step in S14

→↓
Design choice

In year 1, operators enter every change by hand, so they keep their hands-on feel for the plant.

Skill fade happens quickly

Center for Operator Performance / Lamar University
Source shows

In simulator tests, performance fell ~10% after 1 week of advanced process control and nearly 50% after 2 weeks S15 Trade press

→↓
Design choice

It supports Bainbridge’s warning and our year-1 manual-entry rule. We read the size of the effect as a warning sign, not a precise estimate.

CaveatParticipants were engineering students, not plant operators.

Standards

Is it safe and measurable?

Three established practices set how the optimiser’s limits are changed, how it connects to the plant, and how savings are proven.

System flow → slide 2

Management of change

OSHA PSM · 29 CFR 1910.119(l)
Source shows

Process changes need written management-of-change procedures covering the technical basis, the safety and health impact, changes to operating procedures, and authorisation S16

→↓
Design choice

The optimiser’s limits are set and changed only through management of change, signed off by engineers.

NoteThis US rule is used as a good-practice reference. An Indian site would apply its own regulations.

A one-way data link

Data diodes / unidirectional gateways
Source shows

Data diodes enforce one-way transfer in hardware, for example copying the plant historian to the IT network with no inbound path back to the plant S17 Vendor

→↓
Design choice

The AI reads plant data through a read-only link. Nothing can be written back to the control system over that path.

Proving the savings

IPMVP · Efficiency Valuation Organization
Source shows

Savings can’t be metered directly. They are found by comparing measured use before and after, adjusted for changed conditions S18

→↓
Design choice

A weekly savings baseline corrected for production load and weather, so the optimiser is judged on real savings, not on a busy or quiet week.

Alternatives

Why not the other four?

The evidence only, with the condition that would change our mind on each.

Scores and switch conditions → slide 1
A

Predictive maintenance

Runner-up · year-2 bet

Mature programmes are credited with 30–50% less downtime (McKinsey 2017, via a secondary summary) S20 Secondary

But McKinsey (Brink et al., 2021) describes a ~10% false-positive rate wiping out the savings, and notes that many failures are essentially random S19. Plant X also has sensors on only 40% of its equipment and incomplete records S23

Would change our mind if

Sensor coverage and maintenance records were already good enough to train on, or breakdown losses grew larger than the energy prize.

B

Autonomous inspection robots

Not now

The technology is real. BP deployed Boston Dynamics’ Spot on its Mad Dog platform in Oct 2020 for rounds, gauge reading and leak detection S22 Trade press ANYbotics’ ANYmal X is ATEX/IECEx certified for Zone 1 S21 Vendor

But the brief says it is the most expensive option and needs site-specific safety certification S23. Fixed sensors are a cheaper first step.

Would change our mind if

Plant X had a recent serious incident in a hazardous zone. Safety would then outrank cost.

C

AI vision quality inspection

Not now

Image data already exists, but defects cost relatively little, and quality is not one of Plant X’s four pressures S23

Would change our mind if

Defects started to carry a real cost, such as customer complaints or lost orders, or quality became a stated pressure on the site.

D

Plant-wide digital twin

Later

The brief gives it an uncertain payback over 3+ years and a dependence on many data systems S23. With sensors on only 40% of equipment, the data foundation isn’t there yet.

Would change our mind if

Sensor coverage and data integration were already in place and a shorter, credible payback could be shown.

Open questions

What we still don’t know

Four things the published evidence cannot answer for Plant X. The pilot is designed to answer them before more money is committed.

Pilot plan and day-90 gate → slide 3
U1

Will Plant X’s operators accept it?

The research on misuse, disuse and skill fade S12S14 tells us what can go wrong, not how this crew will respond.

Pilot testEvery recommendation is logged as accepted or rejected, with a reason, and measured against the ≥70% acceptance assumption (A4).

U2

Does 4% hold here?

Published results range widely and are not directly comparable to Plant X S1S5

Pilot testSavings are measured against a baseline adjusted for load and weather S18, starting in shadow mode before any change reaches the plant.

U3

Is the historian data good enough?

Plant X has incomplete records and partial sensor coverage S23. Gaps, faulty tags or drifting meters would mislead the model.

Pilot testA data-quality audit of the utility tags comes before any model is trusted. Bad tags are fixed or excluded.

U4

Will the model drift as equipment ages?

Ageing equipment is one of Plant X’s four pressures S23. A model trained on today’s plant may be wrong about next year’s.

Pilot testPredicted and measured outcomes are compared every week. A growing gap triggers retraining, and low-confidence moves are dropped S1
